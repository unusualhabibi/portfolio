The Lagos Court of Arbitration: Africa's Answer to London and Paris?

📅 February 2025
⏱ 7 min read
🏷 LCA · Commercial Arbitration · Africa

Introduction: The Dream of an African Arbitral Seat

For decades, major commercial disputes arising from African transactions — oil and gas contracts, infrastructure projects, mining concessions, banking disputes — have been resolved not on the African continent but in London, Paris, Geneva, or Singapore. This is not simply a matter of legal tradition; it reflects a systematic lack of confidence in African arbitral institutions, African-seated arbitrations, and African courts as supervisory jurisdictions.

The Lagos Court of Arbitration (LCA) was established in 2009 with precisely this problem in mind: to provide a world-class arbitral institution on African soil, capable of administering complex international commercial disputes to a standard that commands the confidence of global commercial parties. Fifteen years on, this essay evaluates how far that ambition has been realised.

Institutional Architecture

The LCA is a non-governmental arbitral institution established by the Lagos Court of Arbitration Law No. 5 of 2009, a Lagos State law. It operates under its own procedural rules — the LCA Arbitration Rules — which were comprehensively revised in 2021 to align with international best practice. The LCA has a Secretariat, a Board of Directors, and an international panel of arbitrators. Its rules permit parties to nominate arbitrators from outside the panel.

The 2021 Rules introduced several significant innovations: emergency arbitrator provisions allowing urgent interim relief before a full tribunal is constituted; an expedited procedure for lower-value or time-sensitive disputes; express provisions for virtual hearings; and provisions for third-party funding disclosure. These changes brought the LCA's procedural framework in line with leading institutions like the LCIA and ICC.

Caseload and Commercial Reality

The LCA has handled several hundred cases since its establishment, spanning construction disputes, financial services, oil and gas, and commercial contracts. However, a honest assessment must acknowledge a persistent challenge: the majority of major Nigerian commercial disputes — particularly those involving international counterparties — continue to be arbitrated in London under LCIA or ICC rules.

This is partly a contractual legacy issue: many existing agreements specify London as seat and LCIA rules before the LCA had established its reputation. But it also reflects a network effect: international commercial lawyers recommend the seats and institutions they know. Changing this requires sustained effort to place LCA-arbitrated awards before international enforcement courts and demonstrate enforceability.

The enforceability test: Under the New York Convention on the Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards, to which Nigeria is a party, an LCA award made in Lagos should be enforceable in any of the Convention's 172 member states. In practice, enforcement track record matters enormously to choosing parties. The LCA needs to systematically document and publish its enforcement successes.

Benchmarking Against LCIA and ICC

A side-by-side comparison on key institutional metrics reveals both progress and persistent gaps.

Speed: The LCA's rules target a final award within 6 months of the tribunal's constitution in expedited proceedings, and 18 months in standard proceedings. The LCIA's 2023 statistics show average time to final award of approximately 19 months. The LCA's targets are competitive — but targets and outcomes are different things, and the LCA does not publish detailed statistics on actual time to award.

Cost: LCA administration fees are generally lower than LCIA or ICC fees for equivalent disputes. For a $5 million dispute, LCA fees would be significantly lower. This is a genuine competitive advantage, particularly for intra-African disputes where both parties may be cost-conscious.

Transparency: The LCIA publishes annual reports with detailed caseload statistics, nationality of parties, industry sectors, and average costs. The ICC publishes similar data. The LCA does not yet publish comparable statistics. This is a significant gap — institutional transparency is part of what builds the confidence that attracts cases.

The Path Forward

The LCA has the institutional framework and the legal infrastructure to become Africa's leading arbitral institution. What it needs now is strategic positioning:

  • Publish comprehensive annual statistics on caseload, time to award, nationality of parties, industry sectors, and enforcement outcomes.
  • Cultivate relationships with international law firms — particularly those with significant African practice groups — to ensure LCA clauses are recommended in new contracts.
  • Develop specialist rules for industry sectors with high dispute volume in Africa: oil and gas, infrastructure, energy, and telecoms.
  • Establish a voluntary publication system for anonymised LCA awards to build jurisprudence and demonstrate quality of reasoning.
  • Work with other African institutions — NCIA in Abuja, KIAC in Nairobi, CRCICA in Cairo — on mutual recognition and referral frameworks to present a coherent African arbitration ecosystem.

Africa does not need to send its disputes to London. But it will only stop doing so when it has institutions that have demonstrably earned the trust that currently goes elsewhere. The LCA is closer to that point than it has ever been.
