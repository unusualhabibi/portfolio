ODR in Nigeria: Can Technology Replace the Courtroom for Small Claims?

📅 December 2024
⏱ 6 min read
🏷 ODR · Access to Justice · Digital Courts

The Access to Justice Crisis

Nigeria's court system is in crisis. The National Judicial Council estimated in 2022 that over 900,000 cases were pending in state and federal courts across the country. In Lagos State alone, commercial disputes routinely take 5–10 years from filing to final judgment. For a ₦500,000 trade dispute or a ₦200,000 landlord-tenant disagreement, this timeline makes litigation economically irrational. The cost of lawyers, court fees, and lost time vastly exceeds the value of the claim. The result: a vast population of genuine legal grievances that simply go unresolved.

Online Dispute Resolution (ODR) — the use of digital platforms to facilitate negotiation, mediation, or arbitration without requiring physical court attendance — offers a potentially transformative response to this access to justice deficit. This essay examines what ODR is, what legal framework currently exists for it in Nigeria, what platforms are emerging, and what barriers need to be overcome.

What ODR Actually Is

ODR encompasses a spectrum of dispute resolution processes conducted through digital platforms. At one end, automated negotiation tools — such as those used by eBay's Resolution Centre, which handles over 60 million disputes annually — apply algorithmic logic to resolve high-volume, low-value standardised disputes. At the other end, fully online arbitrations conducted via video conferencing platforms, with electronic filing of pleadings and digital delivery of awards, use technology as a medium for conducting conventional ADR proceedings.

Between these poles lies a range of hybrid approaches: platforms that offer structured mediation through asynchronous messaging; AI-assisted negotiation tools that suggest settlement ranges based on comparable cases; and online small claims tribunals that allow parties to present their cases without lawyers through guided digital interfaces.

Nigeria's Current Framework

The legal framework for ODR in Nigeria is nascent and partially dependent on the general ADR legislative architecture. The Arbitration and Conciliation Act 2023 (ACA 2023) does not contain express provisions for online arbitration, but its general framework — which recognises arbitration agreements in writing and permits parties to conduct proceedings as they see fit subject to minimum procedural requirements — is broad enough to accommodate virtual arbitration hearings.

Several Nigerian courts adopted virtual hearing protocols during and after the COVID-19 pandemic. The National Industrial Court, the Federal High Court, and some state courts have rules permitting virtual appearances. The Lagos State Judiciary's e-filing system, launched in 2020, allows electronic filing of court processes. These developments create a technological and procedural infrastructure that ODR platforms can build upon.

The enforcement gap: Even if an ODR platform produces a settlement agreement or an arbitration award, enforcement still requires the Nigerian court system. An ODR agreement that cannot be enforced is commercially useless. Bridging the gap between ODR outputs and court enforcement is the single most important challenge for ODR development in Nigeria.

Emerging Platforms and Initiatives

Several ODR initiatives have emerged in the Nigerian market. The Consumer Protection Council has developed a digital complaints mechanism for consumer disputes. Some fintech companies — Flutterwave, Paystack, Kuda — have built internal dispute resolution systems that function as private ODR platforms for payment disputes. The Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (FCCPC) has used digital mediation for competition and consumer complaints.

Private platforms specifically designed for commercial ODR are beginning to emerge. These typically offer a structured negotiation phase, followed by optional mediation with a human mediator, with arbitration as a final backstop. The Nigerian Communications Commission's Complaint Management System handles telecoms disputes digitally — though it functions as a regulatory complaints mechanism rather than a proper ODR platform.

What Needs to Change

For ODR to achieve its potential in Nigeria, several legal and institutional changes are required:

  • Express ODR legislation: The ACA 2023 should be amended, or a standalone ODR Act enacted, expressly recognising the validity of ODR agreements and awards and providing a streamlined enforcement mechanism analogous to the summary enforcement of arbitration awards under Section 57 of the ACA 2023.
  • Court-connected ODR programmes: The Lagos State Judiciary and Federal High Court should develop court-annexed ODR programmes for small claims below a prescribed threshold — say ₦5 million — requiring parties to attempt ODR before filing in court.
  • Digital identity and authentication: ODR platforms require reliable digital identity verification to ensure parties are who they claim to be. Nigeria's NIN system, despite its privacy concerns, provides a national identity infrastructure that could support ODR authentication.
  • Consumer protection standards for ODR platforms: Not all ODR platforms are equal. The FCCPC should develop minimum standards for commercial ODR platforms operating in Nigeria — covering procedural fairness, arbitrator qualification, fee transparency, and data protection compliance.
