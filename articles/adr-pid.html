Nigeria and Investment Treaty Arbitration: Lessons from P&ID v. Nigeria

📅 October 2024
⏱ 9 min read
🏷 ISDS · P&ID · Investment Arbitration

The $10 Billion Wake-Up Call

In September 2019, a London arbitration tribunal issued an award against Nigeria in the amount of approximately $6.6 billion in damages arising from a failed gas processing contract with Process and Industrial Developments Limited (P&ID). With interest, the award had grown to approximately $10 billion by 2020 — equivalent to roughly 20% of Nigeria's foreign exchange reserves at the time. The Nigerian government initially failed to challenge the award within the prescribed timeframe, and enforcement proceedings were commenced in multiple jurisdictions including the United States and England.

The P&ID case was simultaneously a legal disaster and a governance scandal. It revealed multiple failures: in Nigeria's contract management, in its legal representation in the arbitration, in its understanding of ISDS (investor-state dispute settlement) exposure, and in the institutional capacity of the government to respond to international commercial arbitration. This essay analyses the case, its lessons, and what Nigeria has (and has not) done to reform its practices.

What Happened: The Contract and the Arbitration

In 2010, the Nigerian government — through the Ministry of Petroleum Resources — entered into a Gas Supply and Processing Agreement (GSPA) with P&ID, an Irish-registered company. Under the agreement, P&ID was to build and operate a gas processing plant in Calabar; Nigeria was to supply wet gas to the plant. The plant was never built. Nigeria failed to supply the gas it had contracted to provide. P&ID commenced arbitration in London under the GSPA's dispute resolution clause.

Nigeria's conduct of the arbitration was critically deficient. It was represented by under-resourced legal teams, failed to file timely submissions on multiple occasions, did not adequately contest the quantum of damages, and initially failed to challenge the award within the three-month period under the English Arbitration Act 1996. The cumulative effect of these failures — in a forum where procedural discipline is as important as substantive argument — was catastrophic.

"In international arbitration, the award is the finish line. Everything before it — the contract drafting, the factual investigation, the legal strategy, the procedural discipline — determines whether you reach that line on your feet or on your knees."

Nigeria's Eventual Challenge

Nigeria was eventually granted permission to challenge the award in the English courts on the basis of fraud and corruption — arguing that P&ID had obtained the contract through corrupt means and that the arbitration itself had been tainted by corruption. In 2023, a remarkable development occurred: the English High Court (Mrs Justice Cockerill) set aside the award in its entirety, finding that P&ID had indeed obtained the contract through bribery and that the arbitral proceedings had been procured through fraud.

This outcome was a striking vindication for Nigeria — but it should not obscure the underlying lessons. Nigeria won in 2023 on corruption grounds that were available to it from the outset. The question is why it took years of reputational and financial damage before these arguments were properly marshalled and presented.

Systemic Lessons for Nigeria

Contract drafting discipline. The GSPA was a poorly drafted contract that did not adequately define Nigeria's gas supply obligations, did not include a force majeure clause appropriate for a developing country's infrastructure constraints, and included a dispute resolution clause that gave P&ID maximum procedural advantages. Every government contract above a prescribed threshold should be reviewed by qualified commercial lawyers before execution.

ISDS exposure mapping. Nigeria is party to numerous bilateral investment treaties (BITs) and investment protection agreements that expose it to investor-state arbitration. Most Nigerian ministries have no systematic understanding of their BIT exposure. The Office of the Attorney General should maintain a comprehensive register of Nigeria's investment treaty obligations and conduct periodic ISDS risk assessments.

Institutional capacity for arbitration management. The P&ID arbitration was managed by government officials who lacked arbitration expertise. Nigeria needs a dedicated government arbitration unit — analogous to the UK Government Legal Department's international arbitration team — with qualified lawyers who understand international commercial and investment arbitration procedure.

Early legal strategy. The corruption and fraud arguments that ultimately succeeded in the English courts could have been raised far earlier. Nigeria needed to deploy high-quality international arbitration counsel from the outset, with a mandate to investigate and pursue all available defences — including corruption — aggressively.

Reforming Nigeria's Investment Treaty Posture

Beyond the P&ID case, Nigeria needs to fundamentally review its investment treaty architecture. Many of Nigeria's older BITs were signed in the 1990s and early 2000s, during a period when the implications of ISDS provisions were not fully understood. These treaties often contain broad investor protections — fair and equitable treatment, full protection and security, umbrella clauses — that have been interpreted expansively by investment tribunals.

The Nigerian Investment Promotion Commission Act and the new Companies and Allied Matters Act 2020 represent domestic reforms that have modernised Nigeria's investment framework. But domestic law reform does not change treaty obligations. A systematic renegotiation or termination of outdated BITs — replacing them with agreements modelled on the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) Investment Protocol, which contains an ISDS reform agenda — is a long-term priority.
