The Problem of Absent Voices

In August 2024, the European Union AI Act entered into force — the world's most comprehensive binding legal framework for artificial intelligence. Its risk categorisation, prohibited AI practices, governance architecture, and fundamental rights protections will shape how AI is developed and deployed not just in Europe but globally. Companies worldwide will build to EU standards to access the European market. This is the Brussels Effect in action: regulatory standards set in Europe become de facto global standards.

Africa was not at the table when the EU AI Act was drafted. African civil society groups, African governments, and African technology companies had essentially no formal input into a regulatory instrument that will profoundly affect the AI tools deployed on the African continent. The same is true of the US Executive Order on Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy AI (October 2023), China's Measures for the Management of Generative AI Services (2023), and the UK's AI White Paper. The global AI governance conversation has been conducted by a handful of powerful actors, and Africa has been largely absent from it.

This is not a complaint about procedural fairness. It is a substantive warning: AI governance frameworks designed without African input may systematically fail to address African realities, embed assumptions that do not translate to African contexts, and impose compliance burdens on African actors while distributing benefits elsewhere.

The Global AI Governance Landscape

The current global AI governance landscape is fragmented and contested. The EU has taken a risk-based, rights-protective legislative approach through the AI Act. The United States has favoured a more permissive, innovation-first approach, relying primarily on voluntary commitments from major AI companies, sector-specific guidance, and the executive order framework. China has taken a state-guided approach, combining innovation promotion with content control and algorithmic accountability requirements focused on social stability.

These three approaches reflect different political economies, different relationships between state and market, and different conceptualisations of the purpose of AI regulation. None of them was designed with African states, African economies, or African populations primarily in mind. The EU AI Act's risk classification system, for example, designates AI systems used in employment, education, credit scoring, and law enforcement as "high-risk" — requiring conformity assessments, transparency obligations, and human oversight. These designations make sense in a context where such systems are widely deployed by large, resourced institutions. But in Nigeria, where the most consequential AI applications may be in informal lending (mobile credit scoring), agricultural advice (AI-powered extension services), and healthcare diagnosis (AI triage in resource-constrained settings), the EU's risk framework may not map neatly onto the actual risk landscape.

Why Africa's Stakes Are High

Africa's relationship with AI is not abstract. AI systems are already making consequential decisions affecting African lives: credit scoring algorithms determine who gets a mobile loan; facial recognition systems are being deployed by law enforcement agencies; social media content moderation algorithms, trained primarily on English-language data, systematically under-moderate harmful content in African languages while over-moderating legitimate speech.

The last point deserves emphasis. A 2023 study by the University of Washington found that AI content moderation systems were significantly less accurate for content in Swahili, Yoruba, Igbo, and other African languages than for English-language content. This means that African users are simultaneously less protected from harmful content (because harmful content in African languages is not detected) and more subject to false positives (because legitimate content in African languages is misclassified as harmful). This is a direct consequence of building AI systems on training data that does not adequately represent African linguistic and cultural contexts.

"AI systems trained on data from the Global North will reflect the values, assumptions, and blind spots of the Global North. Deploying those systems in Africa without adaptation is not technological progress — it is the digital extension of an old pattern."

Nigeria's AI Policy Position

Nigeria's National Information Technology Development Agency (NITDA) published a National Artificial Intelligence Policy in 2024, and the National Centre for Artificial Intelligence and Robotics (NCAIR) has been established under NITDA to coordinate AI research and development. These are meaningful institutional steps. But Nigeria's AI policy framework is still primarily aspirational — focused on promoting AI adoption and building technical capacity — rather than regulatory.

Nigeria does not yet have binding AI regulations. The NITDA AI Policy contains principles — transparency, accountability, fairness, inclusivity, safety — but does not create enforceable legal obligations. This is appropriate for an early-stage policy framework, but Nigeria needs to develop a clearer regulatory roadmap, including positions on high-risk AI applications, algorithmic accountability requirements, and cross-border data flows for AI training.

Towards an African AI Governance Framework

Africa needs its own AI governance framework — not as a rejection of global standards, but as a contribution to shaping them. An African framework would begin from different foundations than the EU AI Act or the US executive order. It would engage seriously with the philosophy of ubuntu — I am because we are — which conceptualises personhood and rights relationally rather than individualistically. An ubuntu-informed AI ethics framework would emphasise communal impact, collective benefit, and the obligations of AI developers to the communities from which training data is drawn.

Practically, an African AI governance framework would need to address: the bias and accuracy gaps in AI systems trained on non-African data; the governance of AI in healthcare, agriculture, and financial services, where AI tools are most likely to affect the largest number of people; the question of African data sovereignty and the conditions under which African data can be used to train AI models; and the role of African states in international AI governance bodies.

What Must Happen

  • African states must engage formally with international AI governance bodies — the OECD AI Policy Observatory, UNESCO's AI Ethics Recommendation implementation, and the UN's AI governance process under the Global Digital Compact.
  • The African Union must develop a continental AI governance framework — the AU's Data Policy Framework provides a model. An AU AI Governance Framework, developed with civil society and technical expert input, could provide a common African position in international negotiations.
  • Nigeria's NITDA must move from AI policy principles to binding AI regulation, beginning with high-risk applications in credit scoring, healthcare, and law enforcement where AI decisions have the most consequential impacts on Nigerian lives.
  • African governments must invest in AI auditing capacity — the ability to independently assess AI systems deployed in the public sector and in critical infrastructure for bias, accuracy, and rights compliance.

The global AI governance conversation will not wait for Africa. But Africa can still shape it — if it chooses to show up.
