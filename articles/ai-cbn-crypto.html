The 2021 Ban

On February 5, 2021, the Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN) issued a circular directing all banks and financial institutions to close accounts of cryptocurrency exchanges and cease facilitating cryptocurrency transactions. The circular cited concerns about money laundering, terrorism financing, speculative investment risk, and the threat to monetary policy effectiveness. Nigeria joined a small club of countries — including China — that had taken prohibitive action against cryptocurrency at the institutional level.

The immediate practical effect was significant. Nigerian banks closed accounts of entities like Quidax, Luno, and Binance's local operation. Peer-to-peer cryptocurrency trading — which had been Nigeria's dominant method of crypto exchange for years — became the primary remaining avenue for crypto transactions, as it operated outside the banking system the CBN had targeted. The ban did not stop cryptocurrency use in Nigeria; it merely pushed it further into informal channels and made it harder to monitor.

Nigeria was, at the time of the ban, one of the world's largest cryptocurrency markets by peer-to-peer volume. The Chainalysis Global Crypto Adoption Index had ranked Nigeria among the top five countries globally for crypto adoption in 2020. This context makes the ban's logic peculiar — it targeted an activity already so widespread that prohibition was practically unenforceable without a fundamental change in Nigeria's internet infrastructure.

Was the Ban Constitutional?

The constitutional basis for the CBN's 2021 circular was not clearly articulated. The CBN derives its regulatory powers from the Central Bank of Nigeria Act 2007 and the Banks and Other Financial Institutions Act (BOFIA) 2020. These statutes empower the CBN to regulate banking activities and financial institutions — but cryptocurrency exchanges are not banks, and cryptocurrency holdings are not bank deposits. The question of whether the CBN had jurisdiction to prohibit banks from servicing cryptocurrency businesses — as opposed to directly regulating cryptocurrency — was never judicially tested.

Section 39 of the Nigerian Constitution guarantees freedom of expression, which has been read expansively to include economic freedoms in some comparative jurisdictions. Section 44 protects against compulsory acquisition of property. Whether the CBN's instruction to banks effectively constituted a state action that infringed these rights — by making it practically impossible to engage in lawful economic activity — is an interesting constitutional question that remained unlitigated.

The SEC's Parallel Track

While the CBN was banning crypto, the Securities and Exchange Commission was moving in the opposite direction. In September 2020 — five months before the CBN ban — the SEC had issued a Statement on Digital Assets and their Classification, effectively asserting jurisdiction over digital assets that qualify as securities under the Investments and Securities Act 2007. In May 2022, the SEC issued Digital Assets Rules, creating a formal regulatory framework for virtual assets service providers (VASPs), digital asset offering platforms, and digital asset exchanges.

The SEC's approach was risk-based and registration-focused: entities wishing to operate crypto exchanges or issue digital tokens were required to register with the SEC, comply with anti-money laundering requirements, and meet minimum capital requirements. This created a direct conflict with the CBN's prohibition — the SEC was simultaneously creating a regulatory pathway for crypto businesses that the CBN had instructed banks not to service.

The eNaira Experiment

In October 2021 — eight months after banning private cryptocurrencies — the CBN launched the eNaira, Nigeria's Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC). Nigeria was the first country in Africa and one of the first in the world to launch a retail CBDC. The eNaira is a tokenised form of the naira, issued and backed by the CBN, designed to expand financial inclusion and reduce the cost of remittances.

The eNaira's adoption has been modest. By 2023, approximately 13 million eNaira wallets had been created, but active usage remained low. User surveys consistently identified a lack of awareness, limited merchant acceptance, and a preference for existing mobile money solutions (particularly Opay and PalmPay) as barriers to adoption. The eNaira's architecture — which uses a permissioned blockchain controlled by the CBN — is fundamentally different from the decentralized cryptocurrencies the CBN had banned, and this distinction was not always clear to ordinary Nigerians.

The 2023 Reversal

In December 2023, the CBN reversed course. A new circular rescinded the 2021 prohibition and permitted banks and financial institutions to open accounts for VASPs registered with the SEC under the 2022 Digital Assets Rules. The reversal reflected several pressures: the manifest failure of the ban to reduce crypto activity; pressure from the fintech industry and international investors; the growing recognition that regulatory engagement was more effective than prohibition; and the broader economic liberalization agenda of the Tinubu administration.

The reversal was welcomed but left important questions unresolved. Banks remained cautious about opening accounts for crypto businesses — the reputational risk of association with an asset class that the CBN had until recently prohibited, combined with compliance uncertainty, meant that actual bank account openings for crypto entities proceeded slowly in 2024.

Assessing the Current Framework

Nigeria's current crypto regulatory framework is a patchwork. The SEC's Digital Assets Rules 2022 provide the primary regulatory framework for VASPs and digital asset offerings. The CBN's 2023 circular permits banking services for SEC-registered VASPs. The FCCPC has claimed jurisdiction over consumer protection aspects of crypto platforms. The EFCC has jurisdiction over crypto-related fraud and money laundering.

This multi-regulator landscape creates coordination challenges. A crypto exchange operating in Nigeria must satisfy the SEC for its core business, the CBN (indirectly) for banking access, the FCCPC for consumer protection, and potentially SCUML (Special Control Unit against Money Laundering) for AML compliance. A consolidated Digital Assets Act — creating a single regulatory framework administered by a lead regulator with clear inter-agency protocols — would reduce compliance costs and regulatory uncertainty.

Nigeria's crypto journey — from indifference to prohibition to grudging accommodation — reflects a global pattern. The question now is whether Nigeria can become a thoughtful, proportionate, innovation-friendly crypto regulator, or whether its framework will remain reactive and fragmented. The answer will significantly affect whether Nigeria's already substantial crypto ecosystem develops into a formal, investable industry or remains predominantly informal.
