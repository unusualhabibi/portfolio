DAOs as Legal Entities: Can Nigeria Recognize a Decentralized Autonomous Organization?

📅 December 2024
⏱ 7 min read
🏷 DAO · Web3 · CAMA 2020 · Corporate Law

What is a DAO?

A Decentralized Autonomous Organization (DAO) is an entity governed by smart contracts on a blockchain, whose rules are encoded in code rather than in legal documents, and whose decision-making is distributed among token holders rather than concentrated in a board of directors or management team. DAOs have been used for investment funds (MakerDAO, managing billions in DeFi assets), for protocol governance (Uniswap's governance DAO), for collective purchasing (ConstitutionDAO, which raised $47 million in days to bid on a copy of the US Constitution), and for creative collaborations.

The fundamental legal problem with DAOs is that they were designed to operate outside traditional legal structures — and they largely succeed at this. But operating outside legal structures creates enormous practical problems: DAOs cannot hold property in their own name, cannot enter contracts, cannot employ people, cannot sue or be sued, and their members may face unlimited personal liability for the DAO's obligations. The question for every jurisdiction is whether, and how, to accommodate DAOs within existing legal frameworks.

The Wyoming Precedent

In 2021, Wyoming became the first US state to pass legislation specifically recognizing DAOs as a legal entity type. The Wyoming DAO LLC Act allows DAOs to register as limited liability companies, giving them legal personhood while preserving their decentralized governance structure. A DAO LLC in Wyoming can own property, enter contracts, sue and be sued, and its members enjoy limited liability protection — while the DAO's governance structure (voting rights, membership rules, decision procedures) can be defined in its smart contracts rather than in conventional articles of association.

Vermont, Tennessee, and Utah have subsequently followed Wyoming with their own DAO legislation. The Marshall Islands passed the DAO Act 2022 at the national level. These are early and imperfect frameworks — they have generated legal questions of their own — but they represent a genuine attempt to bring DAOs within the legal system without requiring them to abandon their defining characteristics.

CAMA 2020 and the Existing Nigerian Framework

Nigeria's Companies and Allied Matters Act 2020 (CAMA 2020) provides the primary framework for business entity formation. CAMA 2020 recognizes several entity types: companies limited by shares, companies limited by guarantee, unlimited companies, limited liability partnerships, limited partnerships, and business names. It does not recognize DAOs.

Could an existing CAMA entity type accommodate a DAO? The analysis is not encouraging. A company limited by shares could theoretically be structured to approximate DAO governance — using a token-based share structure with weighted voting rights — but CAMA's requirements for a registered address, named directors, company secretary, and conventional articles of association are fundamentally incompatible with a DAO's decentralized, pseudonymous, blockchain-governed architecture.

A limited liability partnership (LLP) is somewhat more flexible — LLPs can define their internal governance entirely through an LLP agreement — but LLPs require identified partners with personal liability exposure in some circumstances, and CAMA's LLP provisions require registration with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC), which requires disclosed personal information that many DAO participants are unwilling to provide.

The liability problem: Without a recognized legal entity structure, a DAO in Nigeria is likely treated as an unincorporated association or a general partnership — meaning its members are personally, jointly, and severally liable for the DAO's obligations. This is commercially untenable for most DAO applications. It creates the perverse situation where decentralization exposes participants to more liability, not less.

Reform Proposals

Nigeria could accommodate DAOs through one of three legislative approaches:

Option 1 — DAO-specific legislation: Following Wyoming, Nigeria could enact a DAO Act creating a new "DAO company" entity type, registered with the CAC, with tailored governance requirements. The registered address and named contact requirements could be simplified; governance documents could incorporate smart contract addresses by reference. This is the most targeted approach but requires new primary legislation.

Option 2 — CAMA amendment: Amend CAMA 2020 to add provisions for "Decentralized Technology Companies" — a new category of legal person with modified requirements for governance, membership disclosure, and registered office. This builds on existing corporate law infrastructure and may be easier to implement than standalone legislation.

Option 3 — SEC Digital Assets Rules integration: The Nigerian SEC's Digital Assets Rules 2022 already regulate some aspects of digital token issuance and exchange. The Rules could be extended to provide a governance framework for DAOs whose tokens are treated as securities, with the SEC providing a registration and oversight function analogous to its role for investment companies.

The Nigerian crypto ecosystem is already substantial — peer-to-peer transaction volumes routinely place Nigeria among the top global markets for crypto activity. DAOs are an emerging part of this ecosystem. Providing them with a clear legal framework would make Nigeria more attractive as a hub for Web3 activity and reduce the liability exposure of Nigerian participants in global DAO structures.
