GDPR vs NDPA: What Nigeria Can Still Learn from the EU's Data Law Journey

📅 October 2024
⏱ 10 min read
🏷 GDPR · NDPA · Comparative · Adequacy

Why Compare?

Nigeria's NDPA 2023 was deliberately modelled on the GDPR — its drafters said so explicitly. The structure of the Act, its core principles (lawfulness, fairness, transparency; purpose limitation; data minimisation; accuracy; storage limitation; integrity and confidentiality), and many of its specific provisions are directly traceable to the GDPR's text. This means comparing the two instruments is not an exercise in abstract legal comparison — it is an examination of how faithfully the model was followed, where it was adapted, and what lessons Nigeria can still draw from nearly a decade of GDPR implementation experience.

The GDPR came into force in May 2018. By 2024, European data protection authorities had issued over €4.5 billion in fines, developed an enormous body of regulatory guidance, and confronted problems — AI, health data, children's data, political advertising — that the drafters of the Regulation did not fully anticipate. Nigeria is entering this landscape with the NDPA. It would be foolish not to learn from Europe's experience.

Where the NDPA Follows the GDPR Faithfully

The NDPA's core data subject rights — access, rectification, erasure, portability, objection — closely track GDPR Articles 15–22. Its data breach notification provisions, while weaker on timelines, follow the GDPR's notification architecture. Its mandatory DPO requirements and data processing agreement obligations are modelled on GDPR Articles 37–39 and Article 28 respectively.

The NDPA's lawful basis framework — Section 25 lists consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task, and legitimate interests as lawful bases for processing — mirrors GDPR Article 6 almost verbatim. This is appropriate: the GDPR's lawful basis framework is one of its most technically sophisticated contributions, and adopting it wholesale saves Nigeria from having to reinvent an already well-developed wheel.

The Right to Erasure: A Comparative Study

NDPA Section 34 provides a right to erasure (sometimes called the "right to be forgotten") — the right to request that a controller delete personal data where it is no longer necessary, where consent has been withdrawn, or where the data has been unlawfully processed. This mirrors GDPR Article 17.

The practical question is how this right can be enforced. Under the GDPR, a data subject can complain to their national supervisory authority (e.g. the ICO in the UK, or the CNIL in France), which has legal powers to investigate and order compliance. Under the NDPA, complaints can be made to the NDPC. But the NDPC's complaints handling infrastructure is still being built. There is currently no published turnaround time for complaints, no published decision register, and no clear escalation pathway to court. Until these processes are established, the right to erasure in Nigeria exists in law but lacks practical enforceability machinery.

Breach Notification: A Significant Divergence

GDPR Article 33 requires notification of a personal data breach to the supervisory authority "without undue delay and, where feasible, not later than 72 hours." NDPA Section 40 requires notification "as soon as reasonably practicable" — a standard that is vaguer and affords controllers more discretion. The 72-hour requirement is demanding in practice — many organisations struggle to meet it — but it creates a clear, auditable benchmark. The NDPA's formulation will require regulatory guidance to give it practical effect.

The Adequacy Question

An EU adequacy decision — a formal European Commission determination that Nigeria provides an adequate level of data protection — would allow the free flow of personal data from the EU to Nigeria without supplementary safeguards. This matters enormously for Nigerian fintech companies, outsourcing providers, and any business handling EU-origin data.

For an adequacy decision, the EU would assess, among other things: the content of Nigeria's data protection law (the NDPA), the independence and effectiveness of the NDPC, Nigeria's international commitments on data protection, and — critically — the government surveillance framework. The last point is Nigeria's most significant obstacle. The EU's adequacy assessments since the Schrems II judgment (CJEU, 2020) give heavy weight to government access to data. Nigeria's current surveillance framework — broad Cybercrimes Act powers, no judicial warrant requirements, no independent oversight — makes an adequacy decision unlikely until substantial reforms are made.

Five Specific Lessons for Nigeria

  • Publish regulatory guidance early. The GDPR's implementation was hampered by delayed guidance from national DPAs. The NDPC should prioritise publishing binding guidance on consent, legitimate interests, and cross-border transfers before enforcement actions begin.
  • Invest in the children's data framework. GDPR Article 8 and the UK's Children's Code represent some of the most practically impactful data protection developments of recent years. Nigeria's NDPA is weak on children's data — a gap that needs urgent attention given Nigeria's large and online-active youth population.
  • Develop an AI-specific guidance note. The GDPR's interaction with AI — particularly automated decision-making under Article 22 — has generated enormous regulatory and litigation activity. The NDPA's provisions on automated processing are sparse. As AI tools proliferate in Nigerian banking, healthcare, and employment, specific guidance is essential.
  • Create a formal adequacy assessment track. Nigeria should proactively engage with the European Commission on adequacy, understanding the specific reforms (particularly in surveillance law) that would need to be made. Adequacy would be worth billions to Nigeria's digital economy.
  • Take enforcement seriously from the start. The GDPR's first years saw many national DPAs reluctant to bring major enforcement actions. This sent the wrong signal. The NDPC's 2024 action against Meta was an encouraging start — maintaining this enforcement energy across a wide range of actors, including Nigerian companies, is essential to regulatory credibility.
