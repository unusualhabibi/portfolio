From NDPR to NDPA: The Legislative Journey

Nigeria's data protection journey is a relatively short one. The Nigeria Data Protection Regulation (NDPR) 2019 — issued as a subsidiary instrument by the National Information Technology Development Agency (NITDA) — was the country's first comprehensive data protection framework. It was, by any measure, a significant step: it established consent requirements, data subject rights, and cross-border transfer restrictions. But it was always a regulation, not an Act of the National Assembly, and its institutional home — NITDA, a technology promotion agency — was always an awkward fit for a data protection regulator.

The Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 (NDPA) addressed this structural problem. Signed into law by President Bola Tinubu in June 2023, the NDPA elevated data protection from a subsidiary instrument to primary legislation, and established a dedicated regulator — the Nigeria Data Protection Commission (NDPC) — with statutory independence. This essay offers a critical assessment of what the NDPA achieves, where it falls short, and what it means for controllers, processors, and data subjects in Nigeria.

What the NDPA Gets Right

Statutory basis and institutional independence. The single most important improvement in the NDPA over the NDPR is its status as an Act of the National Assembly. This means it cannot be amended by ministerial regulation; it requires legislative action to change. The NDPC, established under Part II of the Act, has a governing council with statutory appointment procedures and functional independence from NITDA and the Ministry of Communications.

Expanded data subject rights. The NDPA provides a more comprehensive articulation of data subject rights than the NDPR. Section 34 provides a right to erasure; Section 35 provides a right to data portability; Section 38 provides a right to object to processing. These rights, while modelled on the GDPR, are a genuine advance for Nigerian data subjects.

Data Protection Officers. Section 32 of the NDPA introduces mandatory Data Protection Officer (DPO) requirements for organisations processing data at significant scale or processing sensitive personal data. This is a meaningful compliance requirement that the NDPR only partially addressed.

Key improvement: The NDPA's introduction of a standalone regulator — the NDPC — is the most structurally significant change. An effective data protection regime requires a dedicated, well-resourced regulator with genuine independence. Whether the NDPC will be adequately resourced remains to be seen.

Critical Gaps and Weaknesses

Government surveillance exclusion. This is the NDPA's most significant structural flaw. Section 43 provides that nothing in the Act "shall prevent the processing of personal data for national security, public safety, or law enforcement purposes" subject to conditions. These conditions — primarily that processing be "proportionate" and "necessary" — are vague and subject to wide executive discretion. Nigeria's security agencies operate under legislative frameworks (the Cybercrimes Act 2015, the National Security Agencies Act) that contain no meaningful data minimisation or proportionality requirements. The NDPA does nothing to change this. Government surveillance — which disproportionately affects civil society, journalists, and opposition figures — is effectively outside the Act's reach.

Penalty levels. The NDPA's maximum administrative fine is 2% of annual gross revenue for a first violation, rising to 4% for subsequent violations. While this was modelled on the GDPR's structure, Nigerian enforcement context matters: many companies operating in Nigeria have no registered annual revenue in Nigeria at all (they are foreign entities with local branches), making revenue-based fines practically difficult to calculate and enforce. A floor penalty in absolute terms (as GDPR also provides) would strengthen deterrence.

Consent requirements for existing data. The NDPA introduced a transitional provision requiring controllers who had processed data under the NDPR framework to re-document their legal basis. In practice, many SMEs — the vast majority of Nigerian data controllers — lack the compliance capacity to do this, and the NDPC has not yet issued clear guidance on the transitional requirements.

GDPR Benchmarking

The GDPR remains the global benchmark for data protection law. A comparison with the NDPA reveals both progress and persistent gaps.

  • Breach notification: GDPR requires notification of a data breach to the supervisory authority within 72 hours. The NDPA requires notification "as soon as reasonably practicable" — a weaker, more subjective standard that will generate compliance uncertainty.
  • Data Protection Impact Assessments: GDPR Article 35 requires DPIAs for high-risk processing. The NDPA introduces a similar requirement under Section 33, but the guidance on what constitutes "high-risk" processing has not yet been issued by the NDPC.
  • Adequacy decisions: The GDPR's adequacy decision mechanism allows the EU to approve third countries for data transfers without supplementary safeguards. Nigeria has not yet received an EU adequacy decision — this limits Nigerian companies receiving EU personal data and requires reliance on Standard Contractual Clauses or Binding Corporate Rules.

The Enforcement Question

Data protection law is only as effective as its enforcement. The NDPC became operational in 2023, but its enforcement capacity is still being built. The $220 million fine against Meta in 2024 — the largest data protection enforcement action in Nigerian history — demonstrated that the NDPC is willing to act against major players. But a single high-profile enforcement action does not constitute a functioning enforcement regime.

The NDPC needs to develop a published enforcement policy — similar to the UK ICO's regulatory action policy — that sets out how it prioritises cases, what factors it considers in calculating fines, and what compliance processes it will accept as mitigating factors. Without this, enforcement will be unpredictable, which paradoxically makes compliance harder for well-intentioned organisations.

Verdict

The NDPA 2023 is a genuine improvement over the NDPR 2019. Its statutory basis, expanded rights framework, standalone regulator, and DPO requirements represent real progress. But it is not yet a world-class data protection instrument. The government surveillance gap is a serious human rights concern. The enforcement framework needs development. And the NDPC needs resources commensurate with the scale of its mandate.

Nigeria is building its data protection infrastructure in real time. The NDPA provides a foundation. Whether the edifice built upon it will protect Nigerian data subjects — not just in principle but in practice — depends on choices that have not yet been made.
