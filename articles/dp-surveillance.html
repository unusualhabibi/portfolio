The Legal Vacuum Around Government Surveillance in Nigeria

📅 December 2024
⏱ 7 min read
🏷 Surveillance · Privacy · Cybercrimes Act

The Invisible Threat to Data Rights

Nigeria's data protection conversation has, understandably, focused primarily on the obligations of private sector data controllers — companies, fintech platforms, healthcare providers, educational institutions. The NDPA 2023 is largely structured around this private sector framing. But data protection law that stops at the boundary of the state leaves citizens exposed to precisely the entity with the greatest surveillance capacity and the least accountability: their own government.

This essay argues that Nigeria's data protection framework has a structural blind spot — the surveillance activities of state security agencies, law enforcement, and intelligence services. This blind spot is not accidental; it is a deliberate legislative choice that reflects a wider pattern of executive preference for broad security powers over privacy rights.

SIM Card Registration: Mass Surveillance by Another Name

In 2021, the Nigerian Communications Commission mandated the linking of all SIM cards to National Identity Numbers (NINs). By 2023, over 200 million SIM cards had been linked, creating the largest biometric-linked telecommunications database in sub-Saharan Africa. The stated purpose was to reduce telecoms fraud and enable law enforcement identification of criminal actors.

The legal basis for this mass data collection is the Nigerian Communications Act 2003, which empowers the NCC to issue regulations for telecommunications services. The relevant regulations were promulgated without any meaningful data protection impact assessment, without parliamentary debate on the privacy implications, and without the establishment of any independent oversight mechanism. There is no sunset clause. There is no proportionality assessment. There is no right to challenge the linking requirement.

"Every Nigerian who owns a mobile phone has now been enrolled in a biometric surveillance database as a condition of basic telecommunications access. This is not proportionate, and it was done without the scrutiny such a step demands."

The Cybercrimes Act and Interception Powers

Section 38 of the Cybercrimes (Prohibition, Prevention, Etc.) Act 2015 grants law enforcement agencies the power to intercept electronic communications with the approval of the Attorney General of the Federation. This provision was derived from the Budapest Convention on Cybercrime — but without several of the procedural safeguards the Convention contemplates.

The scope of Section 38 is broad. "Electronic communications" encompasses emails, instant messages, social media posts, VoIP calls, and essentially any digital communication. The authorisation requirement — AGF approval — lacks judicial oversight. There is no requirement for a warrant issued by an independent court. There is no obligation to notify the subject of surveillance after the fact (even after the investigation concludes). There is no independent oversight body reviewing the use of these powers.

Section 38 also imposes an obligation on service providers to "install and maintain interception capabilities" — meaning telecommunications companies must build backdoors into their systems. This is a direct, state-mandated weakening of digital security infrastructure, with implications for all users of those systems.

The NDPA's Surveillance Exemption

Section 43 of the NDPA 2023 provides that the Act does not prevent processing of personal data for national security, public safety, defence, or law enforcement purposes, provided such processing is "subject to appropriate safeguards." The phrase "appropriate safeguards" is left undefined. No guidance has been issued by the NDPC on what constitutes an appropriate safeguard for state surveillance processing.

This is not a uniquely Nigerian problem — the GDPR contains a similar exemption under Article 23, and EU member states have struggled to define its outer limits. The European Court of Human Rights has, over decades of jurisprudence under Article 8 of the ECHR, developed a framework for evaluating surveillance: legality, necessity, proportionality, independent oversight, and effective remedy. Nigeria has accepted obligations under the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights, which contains analogous privacy protections under Article 5 and the right to liberty under Article 6. The ACHPR's jurisprudence on surveillance, though less developed than the ECHR's, provides a framework Nigerian courts could use.

What Reform Would Look Like

A rights-consistent surveillance framework for Nigeria would require, at minimum:

  • Judicial warrant requirements for all electronic interception of communications content (as distinct from traffic data), issued by an independent court applying a probable cause or reasonable suspicion standard.
  • An independent oversight body — analogous to the UK's Investigatory Powers Commissioner — with the authority to inspect, audit, and publicly report on the use of surveillance powers.
  • Post-surveillance notification requirements, permitting individuals to challenge surveillance after the fact where national security is not genuinely jeopardised.
  • Mandatory Data Protection Impact Assessments for all new mass surveillance programmes, with public consultation requirements.
  • Proportionality review of existing programmes including NIN-SIM linkage, which should be subject to independent audit.

None of these proposals are radical. They are the baseline standard in functioning constitutional democracies. Nigeria's constitution guarantees privacy under Section 37. The NDPA 2023 purports to give substance to that guarantee. But a data protection framework that leaves state surveillance unreformed has not fulfilled that promise.
