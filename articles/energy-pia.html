Background: Twenty Years in the Making

The Petroleum Industry Act 2021 (PIA) is the most significant piece of energy legislation in Nigeria's history. Its passage in August 2021 ended a 20-year legislative process that had seen multiple failed reform attempts — the Petroleum Industry Bill (PIB) was first introduced to the National Assembly in 2008 and went through multiple iterations before the PIA's eventual enactment. The delays reflected the extraordinary political economy of Nigerian petroleum: an industry that generates approximately 90% of Nigeria's foreign exchange earnings, employs tens of thousands directly, and sustains the revenues of state governments through the FAAC allocation system.

The PIA's ambition was sweeping: to transform Nigeria's petroleum sector from a state-dominated, chronically under-invested industry into a competitive, commercially-oriented sector capable of attracting the long-term capital needed to maintain production levels as global energy markets shift. Whether it has succeeded — three years on — is the question this essay addresses.

Governance Reforms: NNPC Ltd and NUPRC

The PIA's most structurally significant change was the transformation of the Nigerian National Petroleum Corporation (NNPC) — a statutory corporation that simultaneously acted as regulator, equity partner, and commercial operator — into NNPC Limited, a commercial company incorporated under the Companies and Allied Matters Act 2020. This separation of commercial and regulatory functions was long overdue; the conflict of interest inherent in NNPC's dual role had been identified by every serious reform analysis for decades.

NNPC Limited has now published two annual reports, establishing a degree of financial transparency unprecedented in the history of the Nigerian petroleum sector. However, the government retains 100% ownership of NNPC Limited through the Ministry of Finance Incorporated — a deliberate choice that has attracted criticism. The PIA envisaged a future IPO of up to 30% of NNPC Limited's shares, which would create genuine market discipline and minority shareholder accountability. This has not yet occurred.

The Nigerian Upstream Petroleum Regulatory Commission (NUPRC) was established as the upstream regulator, replacing the Department of Petroleum Resources (DPR). The Nigerian Midstream and Downstream Petroleum Regulatory Authority (NMDPRA) replaced the Petroleum Products Pricing Regulatory Agency (PPPRA) for midstream and downstream matters. Both agencies are still consolidating their institutional capacity — building internal expertise, developing subsidiary regulations, and establishing enforcement track records.

The Fiscal Framework

The PIA introduced a new fiscal regime for upstream petroleum operations, replacing the system of production sharing contracts (PSCs), joint venture agreements, and sole risk contracts that had governed the sector under the Petroleum Act 1969 (now repealed). The new framework introduces a royalty-in-kind option, revised profit petroleum splits, and a tax structure designed to incentivise deepwater development while increasing government take in onshore shallow-water operations.

International oil companies (IOCs) have responded cautiously to the new fiscal terms. Shell's divestment from its onshore joint venture assets (SPDC JV), announced in stages between 2021 and 2023, was driven partly by security concerns in the Niger Delta and partly by the fiscal uncertainty of the PIA transition period. TotalEnergies and Equinor have maintained their deepwater positions. The domestic acquisition of divesting IOC assets by Nigerian independents — Seplat, Renaissance, ND Western — has increased the indigenous character of the upstream sector, though questions about operational capacity and environmental liability management remain.

Host Community Development

Chapter 3 of the PIA introduces a mandatory Host Community Development Trust (HCDT) framework, requiring upstream operators to contribute 3% of their actual operating expenditure annually to a trust fund for the benefit of host communities in their operational areas. This replaced the previous Memoranda of Understanding (MoUs) that operators had used — with widely varying effectiveness — to manage community relations.

The HCDT framework has potential but is still in early implementation. Community identification for trust beneficiaries has proven contentious in several areas, particularly where historical boundary disputes exist. The requirement for a Board of Trustees with community representation is positive, but governance capacity at the community level varies enormously. The 3% contribution rate — while a step forward — compares modestly with what communities in comparable producing jurisdictions receive.

Three-Year Assessment

Three years after enactment, the PIA has achieved significant institutional reforms but has not yet delivered the investment surge it was designed to attract. Nigeria's oil production — which had fallen to approximately 1.1 million barrels per day in 2022, well below the 2.5 million bpd OPEC quota — has recovered to approximately 1.5 million bpd by late 2024, but this recovery reflects improved security in the Niger Delta as much as PIA-driven investment.

The subsidiary regulations that the PIA requires — on decommissioning, on environmental remediation, on gas flaring penalties, on petroleum host community development — are still being finalised by NUPRC and NMDPRA. The PIA's transformative potential cannot be realised until the full regulatory framework is in place. By the standards of comparable resource sector legislation, three years is early in the implementation cycle. The PIA's ultimate legacy will be determined over the next decade.
