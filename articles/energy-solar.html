Solar and Wind Licensing in Nigeria: How NERC's Framework Compares to Kenya and South Africa

📅 September 2024
⏱ 7 min read
🏷 Renewable Energy · NERC · Kenya · South Africa

Why Licensing Frameworks Matter

A country's renewable energy licensing framework is, in practice, the front door of its energy transition. An investor considering a 50 MW solar park in Nigeria, Kenya, or South Africa will ask the same questions in each jurisdiction: How long does it take to get a generation licence? What are the conditions? What grid connection rights do I have? What tariff mechanism will apply? Can I sign a bankable power purchase agreement? The answers to these questions — embedded in licensing regulations, grid codes, and procurement frameworks — determine where capital flows.

This comparative analysis evaluates Nigeria's renewable energy licensing framework against those of Kenya and South Africa — two African countries that have attracted significantly more renewable energy investment per capita than Nigeria despite having smaller economies and less natural resource endowment.

Nigeria: NERC's Licensing Framework

Under Nigeria's current framework, a utility-scale renewable energy generator (above 1 MW) requires a Generation Licence from NERC. The application process involves a feasibility study, technical and financial capacity assessment, environmental impact assessment, land acquisition documentation, and grid connection assessment. NERC's published processing time for a generation licence is 90 days, but in practice, licensing processes routinely take 12–24 months — partly due to the EIA requirement, which is processed by the Federal Ministry of Environment on a separate timeline.

Grid connection is a separate and often more challenging process. Nigeria's transmission grid is managed by the Transmission Company of Nigeria (TCN), which processes connection applications independently of NERC licensing. Coordinating NERC licensing and TCN connection approval is a source of significant investor frustration. There is no single-window or one-stop-shop for renewable energy project approvals.

Nigeria's procurement framework for utility-scale renewables has centred on the Nigerian Electricity Supply Industry (NESI)'s Renewable Energy Feed-In Tariff (REFIT) programme and the Eligible Customer framework, which allows large industrial customers to contract directly with generators. The REFIT programme has attracted some investment but its tariff levels have been subject to regulatory uncertainty.

Kenya: EPRA and the Standard PPA

Kenya's Energy and Petroleum Regulatory Authority (EPRA) administers a licensing framework that has been deliberately designed to reduce investment friction. Kenya introduced a Standard Power Purchase Agreement (SPPA) in 2017 — a government-approved template PPA for utility-scale renewable energy projects that provides investors with a bankable, predictable revenue stream without the need to negotiate bespoke terms with KPLC (Kenya Power and Lighting Company). The SPPA significantly reduces transaction costs and timelines.

Kenya's single-window permitting system for energy projects — the National Environmental Management Authority (NEMA) and EPRA are required to coordinate their review processes — has reduced effective approval timelines. Kenya has also developed a specialised renewable energy tariff schedule under its Scaling Solar programme (developed with IFC), which provides competitively priced grid-connected solar through a reverse auction mechanism with government credit support.

South Africa: REIPPPP as a Model

South Africa's Renewable Energy Independent Power Producer Procurement Programme (REIPPPP), launched in 2011, is widely regarded as the most successful renewable energy procurement programme in sub-Saharan Africa. Over five bid windows, REIPPPP attracted approximately $14 billion in private investment and procured approximately 6.3 GW of renewable capacity. Its success rested on several design features that Nigeria's framework lacks.

First, REIPPPP used a competitive auction mechanism with government-backed PPAs — investors bid a tariff, the lowest bids win, and the PPA is backed by a government guarantee through the Development Bank of Southern Africa. This provided investor certainty. Second, REIPPPP had a strong local content requirement — investors were required to source a minimum percentage of components locally, creating domestic industry linkages. Third, the programme had a dedicated project preparation support facility — the Infrastructure Investment Programme for South Africa — that helped developers navigate the approval process.

What Nigeria Should Adopt

  • A Standard PPA modelled on Kenya's SPPA — government-approved, bankable template terms that reduce transaction costs and negotiation timelines.
  • A competitive auction programme modelled on REIPPPP — with government PPA backing, clear local content requirements, and a dedicated project support facility.
  • Single-window permitting — NERC, TCN, the Federal Ministry of Environment, and state land authorities should be required to coordinate through a single project approval platform for renewable energy projects above a prescribed threshold.
  • Published processing timelines with sanctions — regulatory agencies should be required to process applications within published timelines, with deemed approval provisions or compensation mechanisms for delays beyond the agencies' control.
