Nigeria's Energy Transition Plan: A Legal Analysis of the Regulatory Gaps

📅 November 2024
⏱ 8 min read
🏷 Energy Transition · NDC · Climate · NERC

The Ambition and the Gap

Nigeria's Energy Transition Plan (ETP), launched at COP26 in Glasgow in November 2021 and formally published in 2022, is an ambitious document. It commits Nigeria to achieving net-zero emissions by 2060, to electrifying 25 million homes through off-grid solar by 2030, to growing the country's installed renewable energy capacity from approximately 2 GW to 30 GW by 2030, and to phasing out domestic cooking with biomass by 2030. The ETP is backed by an estimated financing requirement of $410 billion over its implementation period.

These are extraordinary commitments for a country where 85 million people lack access to electricity, where natural gas provides the backbone of power generation, and where petroleum revenues fund approximately 55% of the federal budget. The gap between the ETP's ambitions and Nigeria's legal and regulatory infrastructure is the subject of this essay.

The Paris Agreement Obligations

Nigeria ratified the Paris Agreement in 2017. Its Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) — updated in 2021 — commits to an unconditional reduction of greenhouse gas emissions of 20% below business-as-usual by 2030, and a conditional reduction of 47% subject to international financial support. These NDC commitments are political and diplomatic obligations, not legally enforceable domestic legal duties. Nigeria's constitution does not give the Paris Agreement direct domestic effect; it requires implementing legislation.

The Climate Change Act 2021 represents Nigeria's attempt to give domestic legal effect to its Paris Agreement obligations. The Act establishes a framework for low greenhouse gas emission development, creates the National Council on Climate Change (chaired by the President), and requires the preparation of a Climate Change Action Plan. However, the Act does not establish binding emissions reduction targets in domestic law — it creates a planning framework, not an enforcement mechanism. This is a critical weakness: without legally binding domestic targets, there is no basis for civil society to litigate against government inaction, as has occurred in the UK and Netherlands.

The Electricity Act 2023: Progress and Problems

The Electricity Act 2023 — which replaced the Electric Power Sector Reform Act 2005 — is the most significant electricity sector legislation in two decades. Its most consequential provision is the decentralisation of electricity regulation: states may now establish their own electricity markets and issue distribution and generation licences for intra-state electricity operations, outside the NERC regulatory framework.

For the energy transition, this decentralisation has ambiguous implications. On the positive side, it creates space for states to develop more aggressive renewable energy programmes tailored to local conditions — Lagos and Kano have very different renewable resource profiles. On the negative side, it risks creating a fragmented regulatory environment in which investors face twelve different state regulatory frameworks instead of one national framework. The transaction costs of navigating multiple regulatory regimes could deter the large-scale investment the energy transition requires.

Key tension: The energy transition requires massive, coordinated, long-term infrastructure investment — which requires regulatory predictability and national consistency. The Electricity Act 2023's decentralisation creates flexibility but also fragmentation. Nigeria needs a clear framework for resolving conflicts between state and federal electricity regulation.

NERC and Renewable Energy Regulation

The Nigerian Electricity Regulatory Commission (NERC) has issued a series of regulations relevant to renewable energy development: the Mini-Grid Regulations 2016 (revised 2022), the Embedded Generation Regulations, the Net Metering Code, and various guidelines on renewable energy procurement. This is a reasonable regulatory toolkit — but its implementation has been inconsistent, and several of the frameworks have design flaws that constrain investment.

The Mini-Grid Regulations, for example, contain a provision that allows NERC's distribution companies (DisCos) to acquire a developer's mini-grid at a regulated price if the national grid extends to the mini-grid's service area. This "buyout" provision creates investment uncertainty that has chilled mini-grid development in areas adjacent to existing grid infrastructure — precisely the areas where mini-grids could provide the most cost-effective bridge to full electrification.

The Financing Gap and Its Legal Dimensions

The ETP estimates $410 billion in required investment over its implementation period. Nigeria's current track record in attracting renewable energy investment is modest. Part of this is macroeconomic: currency volatility, foreign exchange availability, and the naira's depreciation create risk profiles that make long-term infrastructure investment unattractive without substantial risk mitigation.

Legal mechanisms for risk mitigation exist — government guarantees, partial risk guarantees through the World Bank's MIGA facility, the InfraCredit guarantee platform — but they are not deployed at scale. The Nigerian government's track record on honouring sovereign guarantees (the Power Purchase Agreement guarantee obligations accumulated under the privatisation of the electricity sector remain partially unpaid) creates reputational risk that deters investors even where guarantees are available.
