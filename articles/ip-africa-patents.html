Why Africa's Patent Problem is Really a Policy Problem

📅 September 2024
⏱ 5 min read
🏷 Patent · ARIPO · Africa · Policy

The Numbers Are Damning

African inventors file fewer than 1% of global patent applications annually. The continent accounts for roughly 17% of the world's population and produces innovators across agriculture, medicine, engineering, and digital technology — yet its contribution to the formal global patent system is negligible. This is not a creativity deficit. It is a systemic failure.

The patent system is supposed to serve a simple social bargain: in exchange for disclosing your invention to the public, the state grants you a limited monopoly to exploit it commercially. This incentivises disclosure and, over time, builds a shared body of technological knowledge. Africa largely does not participate in this bargain — not because its innovators are unwilling, but because the system makes participation prohibitively costly and administratively hostile.

The Cost Barrier

Filing a patent in Nigeria through the Commercial Law Department costs between ₦50,000 and ₦150,000 in official fees alone — before attorney fees, which for a quality patent application can run to ₦500,000 or more. For a university student inventor or a small-scale agricultural innovator in Kano, this is simply not accessible.

The African Regional Intellectual Property Organisation (ARIPO), which covers 22 member states including Nigeria, offers a centralised filing system — the Harare Protocol on Patents and Industrial Designs. But ARIPO fees, while lower than filing separately in multiple jurisdictions, remain out of reach for most individual inventors. The system was designed for corporations, not for the informal innovators who constitute much of Africa's inventive class.

The Institutional Capacity Problem

Even where applications are filed, the institutional capacity to examine them is severely constrained. Nigeria's patent office processes applications extremely slowly — examination backlogs of 3–5 years are not uncommon. This makes patents commercially useless for fast-moving sectors like software or agricultural technology, where a market window may close long before a patent is granted.

"A patent that arrives five years after your product has entered — and possibly left — the market is not a business asset. It is a historical document." This is the reality for many Nigerian inventors navigating the current system.

The TRIPS Trap

The WTO's Agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights (TRIPS) requires member states, including Nigeria, to maintain patent systems meeting minimum international standards. This constrains the policy space available for experimentation. Nigeria cannot, for example, simply create a cheaper, faster "innovation certificate" without potential TRIPS compliance questions.

However, TRIPS does contain significant flexibilities — the Doha Declaration on TRIPS and Public Health famously expanded these for pharmaceutical patents. African states have been slower to exploit TRIPS flexibilities in non-health sectors. There is a strong argument that developing countries could use Article 27(3)(b) more aggressively to create innovation-friendly exceptions for traditional knowledge and agricultural innovations.

A Reform Agenda

The reform agenda must work on three levels simultaneously:

  • Fee reform: ARIPO and national patent offices should introduce means-tested fee scales for individual inventors and SMEs, with subsidies funded through a dedicated African IP Development Fund.
  • Examiner capacity: The African Union's Agenda 2063 should include a specific target for patent examiner recruitment and training. Nigeria alone needs several hundred more trained patent examiners to clear its backlog.
  • University–industry linkage: Nigerian universities produce thousands of potentially patentable research outputs annually that never enter the patent system. Every major university should have a Technology Transfer Office with professional IP staff — not just a nominal committee.
  • Traditional knowledge protection: A parallel system — outside formal patent law — is needed to protect Africa's vast traditional knowledge resources from biopiracy. The Nagoya Protocol under the Convention on Biological Diversity provides a partial framework, but domestic implementation has been weak.

The problem is not that Africa cannot innovate. The problem is that the system built to reward innovation was designed by and for others. Fixing it requires political will, institutional investment, and regional coordination — not just legal amendment. The good news is that all three are achievable.
