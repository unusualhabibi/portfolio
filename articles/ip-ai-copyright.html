Introduction

In 2023, a Nigerian musician used a generative AI tool to produce the instrumental track for a song that subsequently went viral across West Africa. The AI — trained on thousands of existing compositions — produced a wholly original-sounding piece in under four seconds. The question that should have followed, but largely did not, is a deceptively simple one: who owns the copyright in that music?

As generative AI tools flood the creative market — from DALL·E producing visual art to Claude drafting legal memoranda to Sora generating cinematic video — Nigerian copyright law stands conspicuously silent on the question of authorship by non-human entities. This silence is not a minor technical gap. It is an existential challenge to a legal framework whose entire architecture rests on the premise that creative works are produced by human minds.

This essay interrogates the authorship provisions of Nigeria's Copyright Act 2022, evaluates whether existing doctrine can accommodate AI-generated works, surveys how other jurisdictions have approached the problem, and proposes a legislative reform pathway for Nigeria.

The Authorship Requirement Under Nigerian Law

The Copyright Act 2022 — which repealed and replaced the Copyright Act Cap C28 LFN 2004 — remains the primary instrument governing copyright in Nigeria. Section 2(1) of the Act vests copyright in "literary works, musical works, artistic works, cinematograph films, sound recordings and broadcasts." Section 3 specifies the conditions for copyright protection, requiring that a work be "original" and that the author be a "qualified person."

The critical provision is Section 51, which defines "author" to mean, in relation to a literary, musical or artistic work, "the person who creates it." This deceptively simple definition carries enormous weight. The word "person" in Nigerian law — read in conjunction with the Interpretation Act Cap I23 LFN 2004 — encompasses natural persons and, in some contexts, legal persons (bodies corporate). It does not, and has never been construed to, encompass a machine, algorithm, or AI system.

"The author of a work is the person who creates it." — Copyright Act 2022, Section 51. The question is whether an AI system can be a "person" within the meaning of this provision. Current doctrine says no.

This has a cascading consequence. If an AI system cannot be an author, then an AI-generated work — one created entirely by the AI without meaningful human creative input — has no author. And under Nigerian copyright law, a work without an author cannot be protected by copyright. It enters the public domain the moment it is created.

The AI Context: What the Technology Actually Does

To properly evaluate the legal question, it is necessary to understand what modern generative AI actually does. This is not a trivial distinction. There is a meaningful difference between AI as a sophisticated tool (like a camera or a word processor) and AI as an autonomous creative agent.

When a photographer uses a camera, the human makes thousands of creative choices — framing, lighting, timing, subject selection — and the camera executes those choices mechanically. Copyright in the resulting photograph belongs to the photographer. This is analogous to a human using an AI tool to execute a specific, detailed creative vision: the AI is the brush, the human is the artist.

But modern large language models and generative AI systems operate differently in many cases. When a user types "generate a song in the style of Afrobeats about urban loneliness," they have provided a broad prompt. The AI — drawing on billions of parameters trained on existing human creative works — makes the actual creative choices: melody, harmony, rhythm, lyrical structure, metaphor. The human's contribution is the prompt, not the creative expression itself.

Key distinction: Nigerian courts, if confronted with this issue, would likely need to assess the "quantum of human creative input." A highly detailed prompt directing specific creative choices may sustain human authorship. A generic prompt producing a wholly AI-determined output may not. This spectrum of human involvement is something the Copyright Act 2022 is entirely unprepared for.

The Legislative Gap

The Copyright Act 2022 represented a significant modernisation of Nigerian copyright law — it introduced provisions addressing digital rights, expanded the scope of protected works, and strengthened enforcement mechanisms. Yet on the question of AI authorship, it is conspicuously silent. The Act was drafted during a period when generative AI was already commercially available (DALL·E launched in 2021; ChatGPT launched November 2022 — the same year the Act was signed), yet no provision addresses it.

This silence creates three interlocking problems:

  • The Ownership Vacuum: If an AI-generated work has no author, it has no copyright owner. The developer of the AI cannot claim ownership under existing doctrine. The user who provided the prompt may have a weak claim at best. The work is technically public domain.
  • The Investment Problem: Nigerian creative industries — Nollywood, Afrobeats, fashion, literature — are beginning to integrate AI tools. If the outputs of those tools attract no copyright protection, there is no legal incentive to invest in AI-assisted creative production at scale.
  • The Training Data Problem: The Copyright Act 2022 also says nothing about whether training AI models on copyrighted Nigerian works without a licence constitutes infringement. This is a second, equally urgent gap that this essay addresses briefly in the reform section.

Comparative Approaches

Nigeria is not alone in confronting this problem, but it can learn from jurisdictions that have begun to address it.

United Kingdom: The UK's Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 contains what is arguably the world's most AI-forward provision in this area. Section 9(3) provides that for "computer-generated works" — defined as works "generated by computer in circumstances such that there is no human author" — the author is "the person by whom the arrangements necessary for the creation of the work are undertaken." This is typically interpreted as the developer or operator of the AI system. Nigeria could adopt a comparable provision.

United States: The US Copyright Office has taken a more restrictive approach, refusing to register copyright in works produced entirely by AI (see: Thaler v. Perlmutter, 2023). The Office's position is that copyright requires human authorship and that AI is a tool, not an author. This approach protects the public domain but may undermine investment incentives.

European Union: The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689), while primarily a risk-regulation instrument, intersects with copyright through Article 53, which requires providers of general-purpose AI models to publish summaries of training data used — with implications for copyright infringement claims related to training.

Reform Proposals for Nigeria

Based on the foregoing analysis, I propose the following legislative amendments to the Copyright Act 2022:

1. Define "Computer-Generated Work": Amend Section 51 to add a definition of "computer-generated work" modelled on the UK provision — i.e., a work generated by an AI system in circumstances where there is no human author in the traditional sense.

2. Assign Limited Copyright to Operators: For computer-generated works, vest a limited copyright (perhaps 25 years rather than life plus 70) in the person who makes the necessary arrangements for the AI system to create the work — i.e., the operator or user. This preserves investment incentives without creating perpetual monopolies over AI-generated content.

3. Address Training Data: Introduce a provision clarifying that the use of copyrighted works to train AI models requires either a licence or falls within a specific exception subject to opt-out rights by rights holders. The EU's approach under the Digital Single Market Directive (Article 4) is a useful model.

4. Establish a Nigerian Copyright Commission AI Task Force: The NCC should establish a dedicated working group — including technologists, practitioners, and academics — to monitor developments and recommend further amendments. The law will need to evolve as the technology does.

Conclusion

The question of AI authorship is not a distant, speculative problem for Nigerian copyright law. It is an immediate one. Every day that Nigerian musicians, filmmakers, writers, and designers use AI tools without a clear legal framework for ownership, they operate in a vacuum that could expose them to disputes and deprive them of rights they reasonably expect to have.

The Copyright Act 2022 was a commendable step forward. But in its silence on artificial intelligence, it has already fallen behind the technological reality it is meant to govern. Nigeria's creative industries — among the most vibrant on the African continent — deserve a copyright framework that grows with them.
