Introduction

Nollywood is not just a film industry. It is a cultural juggernaut — the world's second-largest film industry by volume, generating an estimated $660 million annually and directly employing over one million Nigerians. Its stars are household names across sub-Saharan Africa. Its storylines shape public discourse. Its aesthetic — vibrant, melodramatic, deeply rooted in Igbo, Yoruba, and Hausa cultural sensibilities — is globally recognisable.

Yet for all its commercial power, Nollywood's relationship with trademark law is surprisingly underdeveloped. Actor names, film titles, production company marks, and franchise characters are routinely exploited by third parties with near-total legal impunity. This essay examines why, and what should change.

Nollywood's Economic Footprint

The Nigerian film industry contributes approximately 2.3% of GDP, according to the National Bureau of Statistics. Streaming platforms — Netflix, Amazon Prime, ShowMax, and African-specific platforms like Africa Magic and iROKOtv — have dramatically expanded Nollywood's global reach. A 2023 Netflix report identified Nigeria as one of its fastest-growing markets for local content production.

This commercial success has created valuable brands. The name "Genevieve Nnaji" carries enormous commercial value. The title "Living in Bondage" is a recognisable franchise. The "Inkblot Productions" mark signals quality to a specific demographic. These are economically valuable intangible assets that trademark law is designed, in theory, to protect.

Nigerian Trademark Law: The Framework

Nigeria's primary trademark legislation is the Trade Marks Act Cap T13 LFN 2004, supplemented by the Trade Marks Regulations. The Act provides for the registration of marks in connection with goods and services, and the registered proprietor of a mark acquires the exclusive right to use it in connection with the registered goods or services.

For trademark protection to exist, registration is typically required. The Commercial Law Department of the Ministry of Trade administers the Trade Marks Registry. Registration grants the holder the right to bring infringement proceedings — an action available where an identical or confusingly similar mark is used without consent in the course of trade.

The Problem: Most Nollywood practitioners — actors, directors, production companies — have not registered their personal or professional names as trademarks. This leaves them with only passing-off claims at common law, which are more expensive and difficult to sustain than registered trademark infringement actions.

Trademark Dilution: The Problem in Practice

Trademark dilution — the weakening of a famous mark's distinctiveness or reputation through unauthorised use — is a recognised concept in jurisdictions with sophisticated IP frameworks (notably the US Lanham Act, Section 43(c)). In Nigeria, the Trade Marks Act does not expressly recognise dilution as a cause of action separate from infringement. This creates a significant gap.

The practical consequences are visible. Merchandisers in Alaba International Market sell T-shirts bearing the likenesses of Nollywood stars without permission. Market traders sell DVDs in packaging that mimics the visual identity of established production companies. Mobile game developers create unofficial games using the names and personas of famous Nollywood characters.

In each case, the harm is not necessarily confusion — consumers may well know these are unofficial products — but rather the dilution of the brand's distinctiveness and the commercial loss of licensing revenue that should flow to the rights holder.

Enforcement Gaps

Even where rights formally exist, enforcement is challenged by several structural factors. First, litigation through the Federal High Court (which has exclusive jurisdiction over IP matters) is expensive and slow — average IP cases take 3–7 years. Second, the Nigerian Copyright Commission and the Trade Marks Registry lack coordinated enforcement capacity. Third, there is no dedicated IP court in Nigeria, meaning IP cases compete for judicial time with a vast backlog of commercial disputes.

The 2022 Copyright Act made meaningful progress — introducing ex parte Anton Piller-style search and seizure orders and strengthening criminal penalties for infringement. But these provisions address copyright infringement primarily, not trademark dilution.

Recommendations

Several targeted interventions could significantly improve the situation for Nollywood practitioners:

  • Amend the Trade Marks Act to introduce an express anti-dilution provision for famous marks, modelled on Section 43(c) of the US Lanham Act or Article 10bis of the Paris Convention on unfair competition.
  • Establish collective registration programmes through bodies like the Association of Movie Producers (AMP) to help practitioners register their marks affordably and collectively.
  • Create an IP Fast-Track Division within the Federal High Court for IP disputes below a prescribed value threshold, reducing litigation time from years to months.
  • Develop right-of-publicity protections — distinct from trademark — protecting individuals' names, likenesses and personas from commercial exploitation without consent. Nigeria has no such statutory right, forcing practitioners to rely on the tort of passing off.

Nollywood's global cultural influence is a Nigerian asset. The legal framework governing it should match its ambition.
